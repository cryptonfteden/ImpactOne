# Sprint 35 Executive Memo
## Office of the Global Product Board — ImpactOne

---

## What Is the Single Biggest Opportunity Hidden Inside the First Five Beta Users?

**The chance to directly, personally watch whether this platform's known trust-critical failure modes actually break a real human's trust — a form of evidence that becomes permanently unavailable the moment those failure modes are fixed.** Every prior audit this year has found the same specific, still-open issue: a confident, specific claim about a user's own portfolio that isn't true for an account with nothing in it. Every one of those findings, so far, came from a reviewer deliberately looking for it. Five real, hand-picked, closely-observed beta users offer something categorically different and irreplaceable — the opportunity to see, in a real person's face and words, in the moment it happens, whether that exact failure is noticed unprompted, and exactly how it lands. That single data point, watched directly rather than inferred, is worth more to `TRUST_RECOVERY_PLAYBOOK.md`'s and `BETA_FEEDBACK_ANALYSIS.md`'s underlying assumptions than a thousand survey responses collected after the fact — because once the issue is fixed, this specific kind of ground-truth observation can never be gathered again.

---

## What Mistake Would Permanently Reduce the Value of That Opportunity?

**Treating five users like five hundred — running the pilot as a light-touch, survey-only exercise, or rushing to expand the cohort before genuinely, closely observing all five.** The entire value of a five-person pilot is depth: the ability to sit with each participant, watch where they hesitate, notice what they never mention unprompted, and see a real reaction to a real trust failure if one occurs. That value is destroyed the moment the team substitutes a distant, asynchronous survey for direct observation, or moves to expand the cohort to twenty-five before every one of the five has actually been watched, not just asked. A team that rushes past this stage gets the same shallow signal a much larger cohort would have given anyway — and forfeits the one thing this specific scale was uniquely capable of providing, permanently, the moment the opportunity has passed.

**The second, related mistake:** quietly patching the known trust issue for this specific cohort ahead of time so it never surfaces, rather than genuinely observing what happens if a real user encounters it under real conditions. Fixing the underlying problem before wider release is correct and necessary. But if it is fixed *silently, specifically to avoid this cohort ever seeing it*, the team has protected five people's experience at the cost of the one irreplaceable piece of evidence this entire memo is about — whether the platform's own worst known failure mode actually breaks trust the way every internal audit has assumed it would. That evidence should be sought deliberately and observed directly, not avoided.
