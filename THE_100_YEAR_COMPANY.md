# The 100-Year Company
## Office of the Chief Customer Officer — ImpactOne

**Mandate:** Describe what decisions ImpactOne must keep making if it wants to deserve user trust one hundred years from now. Not five years — a hundred. At that horizon, no individual product decision matters nearly as much as which *kind* of decision-making survives every leadership change, every market cycle, and every technology shift between now and then.

---

## What a Hundred Years Actually Changes

Every specific thing about this company today — its interface, its underlying models, the five engines, the exact wording of its confidence scores — will be unrecognizable in a hundred years, the same way nothing about how a 1926 financial company operated resembles how one operates today. The only things capable of surviving that much change are *decisions about how to decide*, not any specific decision itself. This document is about those, and only those.

---

## The Decisions That Must Survive Every Leadership Change

**The decision to grade its own mistakes as visibly as its successes, forever, with no expiration date on that commitment.** A hundred years is long enough for at least three or four generations of leadership, each facing a moment where hiding a bad quarter's track record would be easier than publishing it. The company that still exists in a hundred years is the one where every single one of those leaders chose to publish it anyway, because the company's entire value is the unbroken length of that record — and a record broken once, quietly, at any point in a hundred years, calls every year before and after it into question.

**The decision to never let a commercial relationship silently bias what a user is shown.** A hundred years includes many business models this company hasn't tried yet, some of which will create real temptation to let a paid relationship quietly influence a ranking, a recommendation, or a score. The decision that must survive is refusing that trade every single time it's offered, for a hundred years, not just for as long as the founders are in the room to enforce it personally.

**The decision to keep confidence and uncertainty as two separate, honest numbers, no matter how much simpler one blended number would be to explain.** Every generation of product leadership will face pressure to simplify this into one friendlier-looking score. The decision that must survive is refusing that simplification, because it is the one design choice that most directly encodes the difference between a system that knows what it doesn't know and one that has stopped being able to say so.

**The decision to keep the advisory-only boundary structural, not just promised.** A hundred years is long enough for "just this once, just for the users who ask for it" pressure to appear many times, in many disguises, to place a real trade automatically on someone's behalf. The decision that must survive is that this line is never crossed, in any form, under any business justification, no matter how many other companies in the category eventually cross it and no matter how much revenue it appears to leave on the table.

**The decision to keep the free evidence-and-honesty layer free, permanently.** Every future leadership team will, at some point, look at a monetization opportunity that involves gating some piece of the honest core — a confidence score, a track record, an uncertainty figure — behind a paywall. The decision that must survive is that the honesty itself is never for sale, only depth and convenience are.

---

## The Decisions That Must Survive Every Technology Shift

Whatever reasoning technology exists in fifty or a hundred years — vastly more capable than anything available today — the decisions that must govern it are unchanged from the ones that govern today's simpler tools: it must be able to explain itself, it must never claim certainty it doesn't have, it must search for disconfirming evidence as a required step rather than an optional one, and its mistakes must be recorded exactly as permanently as its successes. A hundred-year company does not get to relax these standards because the technology got more impressive — if anything, a more persuasive, more capable system that has not earned proportionally more scrutiny is a bigger risk to trust, not a smaller one.

---

## The Decisions That Must Survive Every Market Cycle

Markets over a hundred years will include booms, crashes, and events nobody today can predict the shape of. What must survive every one of them is the specific discipline described in `TRUST_RECOVERY_PLAYBOOK.md`: surface bad news before being asked, explain it specifically, attribute it honestly, and never use a crisis to extract more attention, engagement, or revenue than the moment's honest facts justify. A company that behaves this way through one crash builds trust. A company that behaves this way through ten, across a hundred years, becomes something closer to an institution than a product — which is the only realistic path to still being trusted at that horizon at all.

---

## The Decisions That Must Survive Every Growth Temptation

At every scale this company will ever reach — a hundred users, a hundred million — there will be a moment where relaxing a trust principle appears to unlock meaningfully faster growth. The decision that must survive every one of those moments, at every scale, forever, is stated plainly in `GROWTH_PLAYBOOK.md` and repeated here because it is the single decision this entire document reduces to: **a growth number achieved by relaxing a trust commitment is not growth. It is a different, worse company wearing this one's name.**

---

## What "Deserving Trust" Actually Means at This Horizon

A hundred years from now, no one will remember this specific interface, this specific model, or this specific team. What could still be true, if every decision above is kept, is that a person a hundred years from now could look at the platform's full, unbroken, published history — including a hundred years of admitted mistakes — and conclude, correctly, that it has never once lied to make itself look better. That is the only inheritance worth protecting across a horizon this long, and it is the only real answer to what this document was written to ask: not what will this company build, but what will it keep refusing to compromise, decision after decision, for a hundred years, so that trust earned in year one is still exactly as valid in year one hundred.
