# Mission Control Design Review — Phase DESIGN-002-REVIEW

**Reviewer role:** Chief Product Officer, UX Director, and Design Critic — reviewing `MISSION_CONTROL_EXPERIENCE_MASTERPLAN.md` as a design specification only. No code was reviewed, no redesign is proposed, and no implementation guidance is offered. Every finding below is a critique of the document as written, evaluated as if Apple, Linear, and Bloomberg were independently reviewing it before launch.

---

## 1. First Impression

**The document never actually designs for 5 seconds — it designs for 10, and fully delivers only at 90.**

Its own stated timeline begins at "0-10 seconds — Orientation" and reaches full comprehension at 90 seconds. Within a true 5-second window, a real user sees: an entrance animation still resolving (a top-down stagger, described as occurring on load), and — per the document's own eye-path narrative — the Top Priority card, which delivers exactly one fact (headline, direction/confidence, one sentence, an attention-level badge). That is a genuine, credible 5-second win for *one* of the three things this review's mission asks about ("what matters"). It is **not** a 5-second win for "what happened" (plural — that's Today's Brief's job, and the doc only promises full absorption of the Brief within "10-30 seconds") or "what affects them personally" (that's Portfolio Intelligence, explicitly placed at the "30-60 second" mark). **Ranked HIGH**: the document should not be read as satisfying a 5-second bar for all three of the mission's named questions — it satisfies one of three within 5 seconds, and this gap is never acknowledged in the spec.

**Does it still feel like a dashboard?** This is the review's most important structural finding, addressed fully in §6 (Premium Feeling) below — the short answer is that the document's own architecture (ten independently-specified, vertically-stacked sections, each with its own elevation/motion/density rules) is, structurally, a very well-organized dashboard, and the spec never directly confronts why "many deliberately-weighted modules in one column" is categorically different from "a dashboard," beyond asserting that the weighting is more deliberate. **Ranked CRITICAL** — this bears directly on whether the finished product will read as an "operating system" or "a modern financial dashboard," which is the mission's own Q6.

## 2. Information Hierarchy

**A genuine, unresolved contradiction exists between section order and the claimed eye-path.**

Section 3.1 (Today's Brief) is placed *above* Section 3.2 (Top Priority) in the document's own vertical flow — Top Priority is literally the second thing on the page. Yet the "0-10 seconds" walkthrough (§6) states flatly: *"The user's eye lands on the single Top Priority card first."* These two claims cannot both be true by default — Western top-to-bottom reading order would land the eye on Today's Brief first, and the document offers no explicit resolution (e.g., "Top Priority actually renders above Today's Brief despite being numbered 3.2," or "elevation/pulse animation is strong enough to override reading-order primacy in every case, and here is why"). §5 (Information Psychology) gestures at an answer — asserting that size/contrast can outrank vertical position — but never applies this reasoning explicitly back to the Brief-vs-Top-Priority ordering it directly contradicts. **Ranked CRITICAL**: the mission's own question 2 asks "is there exactly one visual starting point?" and this document, read literally, describes two different candidates for that starting point without resolving which one wins.

**A second, self-acknowledged hierarchy conflict: Biggest Risk and Best Opportunity are deliberately equal.**

The document explicitly designs these two sections to be "roughly equal in visual weight," "always shown as a paired, comparable set," with symmetry "deliberate and load-bearing." This is a reasonable, defensible design choice (avoiding editorial bias toward pessimism or optimism) — but it is also, by definition, exactly what question 2 asks reviewers to identify: two things "competing for equal attention." The document's opening principle (§2: *"Nothing is equal"*) is directly in tension with this later, deliberate exception, and the tension is never named or reconciled — the document simply asserts both principles in different sections without acknowledging they pull against each other. **Ranked HIGH.**

## 3. Cognitive Load

**The document assumes a vocabulary an "ordinary investor" has not been taught.**

Across the ten sections, the spec relies on platform-specific terms without ever specifying an in-context explanation, tooltip, or first-use teaching moment: "Attention Score," "Confidence," "Recommended Attention Level," "Claim," "Contested Claim," "portfolio relevance," "invalidated," "strengthened/weakened." A genuinely ordinary investor — the mission's explicit persona — does not arrive already knowing what a "Contested Claim" is, or why a numeric "Attention Score" differs from a "Confidence" percentage, or why both accompany a "Recommended Attention Level" badge that appears to restate the same judgment a third way. The document's Failure States section (§9) is admirably rigorous about *disclosing uncertainty* honestly, but nowhere does the specification address *teaching vocabulary* to a first-time or casual user. **Ranked CRITICAL** — this is not a cosmetic gap; the mission explicitly asks whether the screen "requires financial knowledge," and as specified, it requires product-specific literacy the document never accounts for teaching.

**Three overlapping signals per Brief item is real, avoidable redundancy.** Today's Brief items each carry an Attention Score, a Confidence indicator, and a Recommended Attention Level badge (explicitly High/Medium/Low). Two of these three are very likely near-duplicates of the same underlying judgment expressed three different ways (a numeric score, an implied-or-explicit confidence figure, and a discrete label derived from one or both). The document never states why all three are necessary simultaneously at the *lowest*-density section on the whole screen, which is specifically meant to be read "in seconds, not studied." **Ranked HIGH** — this is a direct, addressable instance of unnecessary complexity in exactly the place the document claims should have the least.

## 4. Zero Prompt Principle

This is the document's strongest section. Section 7 explicitly maps every proposed section to the specific question it exists to pre-answer, and the mapping is genuinely disciplined — Today's Brief, Top Priority, Biggest Risk/Best Opportunity, Portfolio Intelligence, Claims Changing, and Market Pulse all correspond to real, named user questions rather than generic "here's some data" modules. **No issue ranked here above LOW** — the one minor gap is that Upcoming Events pre-answers "what's scheduled" but never addresses whether the user is told *what would make that event notable* (e.g., a consensus expectation to compare a result against) — a small completeness gap, not a structural one.

## 5. Emotional Journey

**The intended arc (calm → informed → in control → prepared) is well-reasoned, but one real emotional risk is unaddressed.** The document treats visible belief-revision ("Living Intelligence," Claims strengthening/weakening/invalidating in view) as unambiguously trust-building. For a user early in their relationship with the product — before a real track record has had time to compound — watching the platform visibly reverse itself on a regular cadence could just as plausibly read as *inconsistency* rather than *transparency*. The document doesn't weigh this alternative interpretation at all, despite it being a well-documented risk in trust research for exactly this kind of "showing your work" design. **Ranked MEDIUM-HIGH.**

**A second, smaller risk:** the emotional arc's final act (Market Pulse → Feed → Events → Continue Exploring) is described entirely in terms of decreasing visual energy ("calming down," "flattest," "quietest"). A well-crafted emotional journey usually closes with some form of deliberate resolution, not merely a fade to silence — the document risks the ending feeling like the experience simply *runs out* rather than concludes with intent. **Ranked MEDIUM.**

## 6. Premium Feeling

**The central critique of this whole review:** the document repeatedly asserts Mission Control "must feel like a modern intelligence operating system... not a wall of widgets" — but the specification's own structure (ten sections, each independently assigned its own elevation tier, glass/solid treatment, motion behavior, density, and interaction model) is, by construction, an extremely well-art-directed *list of modules*. Genuinely premium, OS-grade experiences (the reference points the mission itself names — Apple, Linear) typically achieve their feeling through having *very few* distinct visual treatments across a surface, not through meticulously differentiating ten of them. The rigor invested in specifying each section's individual behavior is real craft, but craft applied to justifying ten separate things is not the same as achieving the feeling of one coherent thing. **Ranked CRITICAL** — this is the single most important judgment call in the whole review, and it cuts against the document's own primary ambition.

## 7. Attention Economy

Several concrete candidates for removal, merging, or postponement exist, none of which the document considers:

- **Biggest Risk and Best Opportunity could plausibly be merged** into a single "Key Signals Today" section presenting both sides at once, preserving the intended balance without requiring two fully-specified sibling sections. **Ranked MEDIUM.**
- **"Continue Exploring" likely duplicates existing global navigation.** If the product already has a persistent way to reach Portfolio, Watchlist, Symbol Pages, and AI Analysis (as any real product with more than one screen must), a dedicated exit-navigation section at the bottom of the daily home screen is redundant chrome, not a needed feature — and redundant chrome is exactly what an attention-protecting design should question first. **Ranked HIGH.**
- **The Live Intelligence Feed's placement directly beneath the calmest, most curated content risks re-inviting "scan everything" behavior right after the design's real work (bounding and prioritizing) is already done.** A stricter, more disciplined "zero prompt, calm home screen" might not embed the raw feed inline at all, deferring entirely to a link into the dedicated Daily Feed screen. **Ranked MEDIUM-HIGH.**
- **Market Pulse, Upcoming Events, and Continue Exploring together account for three of the document's ten sections while serving the least-frequent, lowest-urgency needs** — a stricter application of the document's own "postpone what isn't essential" logic would consider collapsing some combination of these behind a single secondary affordance rather than reserving three separate, always-rendered sections of vertical space for them. **Ranked MEDIUM.**

## 8. Personalization

Section 8's specificity is genuinely strong — portfolio-relevance weighting, risk-profile-adjusted thresholds, watchlist-adjacent treatment, and a disclosed tie-break role for learning history are all real, concrete personalization mechanisms, not vague gestures. **The unaddressed gap:** for any user without an established portfolio, watchlist, or interaction history — a very common state for a new user, which is exactly when a first impression matters most — the document's own honest fallback ("pure confidence/market-impact ranking") means personalization substantially collapses, and two different day-one users would very likely see close to the same screen. The document does not confront this cold-start reality anywhere, despite it directly matching the failure mode question 8 asks reviewers to probe for. **Ranked MEDIUM-HIGH** — credit given for the honesty of the fallback design itself, but the gap in addressing the cold-start cohort is real.

## 9. Differentiation

**This is the review's second most important finding.** Stripped of the platform's specific reasoning vocabulary (Claims, Attention Score, confidence/evidence language) and reduced to pure visual structure — a card-based headline list, a paired risk/opportunity callout, a portfolio summary panel, a sentiment gauge, a news feed, and an events calendar — the described layout is architecturally very close to what a personalized Yahoo Finance homepage or a Seeking Alpha "My Portfolio" dashboard already presents today. The document's actual differentiation lives entirely in the underlying reasoning system (Claims, evidence, visible belief revision) and in copy/microcopy discipline — **not in anything a screenshot alone would reveal.** The mission's own test is explicitly a screenshot test ("if screenshots were shown without logos"), and the document never proposes a single distinctive visual primitive (an unconventional layout, a non-card information unit, a typographic signature beyond "Display-sized headline") that would make a bare screenshot unmistakably this product rather than a competitor's. **Ranked CRITICAL.**
