# Customer Journey Map
## Office of the Chief Customer Officer — ImpactOne

**Mandate:** Map the entire relationship a customer has with ImpactOne, from the first visit to a year of use, through the moments that matter most — including the ones no company wants to plan for. A trust-built company is defined less by its best moments than by how deliberately it has thought through its worst ones before they happen.

---

## Stage 1: First Visit

**Expectations:** A visitor arrives skeptical by default — financial products have trained people to expect either confusing jargon or overconfident sales language. They expect to be asked for something (an email, a deposit) before they see any real value.

**Emotions:** Cautious curiosity, mild suspicion, low patience — if the first screen doesn't make sense in seconds, most visitors leave without a second thought.

**Trust risks:** Any claim before evidence. Any confident-sounding number with no explanation. Any request for information before value has been shown (per `MOBILE_PRODUCT_MASTERPLAN.md`'s onboarding design, account creation is deliberately deferred past this point for exactly this reason).

**Opportunities:** The single fastest trust-building move available in this entire journey — showing one real, sourced, honestly-scored insight before asking for anything — happens here or nowhere. A visitor who sees the platform admit uncertainty in its very first interaction has already learned something no competitor's landing page teaches.

---

## Stage 2: First Recommendation

**Expectations:** The customer expects either a confident verdict (what most finance apps give) or a wall of caveats that says nothing useful (what most compliance-driven products give). They do not yet expect a third option: a specific, confident-where-earned, honest-where-not answer with its own stated invalidation condition.

**Emotions:** A mix of hope and doubt — this is the moment a customer decides whether the product is "for real" or "another app that sounds smart." The emotional stakes are higher than any single screen visit later in the relationship, because this is where the category-wide skepticism either gets confirmed or starts to break.

**Trust risks:** A confidence score that doesn't visibly correspond to real evidence strength. A recommendation with no stated horizon or invalidation condition. Anything that reads as templated rather than specific to this exact call.

**Opportunities:** This is the moment to show the full evidence chain, the quality breakdown, and the explicit "this would prove it wrong" condition — not hide them behind an expandable a curious customer might never find. The first recommendation a customer reads is the one they will measure every future recommendation against.

---

## Stage 3: First Month

**Expectations:** The customer expects the product to either fade into background noise (checked once, forgotten) or to escalate into anxiety-driven daily checking (the pattern every existing finance app has trained them to expect). They do not yet expect a calm, short, honest daily habit that sometimes says nothing happened.

**Emotions:** Early trust is fragile and specific — it attaches to particular moments (a graded outcome, an honest "nothing urgent" day, a clear explanation that finally made a confusing concept click) rather than to the product as a whole yet.

**Trust risks:** Inconsistency between screens (a number on one screen that seems to disagree with another). A quiet month with no real engagement from the product beyond the daily brief, leaving the customer wondering if anyone is home. Education content that repeats itself instead of adapting to what the customer has already learned.

**Opportunities:** The first honest "nothing urgent today" is a genuine trust-building event, not a null result — it proves the daily habit isn't manufacturing urgency. The first time a piece of jargon is explained clearly and then stops being explained (because the customer no longer needs it) is a small but real signal the product is paying attention to them specifically.

---

## Stage 4: First Market Crash

**Expectations:** The customer expects one of two failure modes from experience with other products: either false calm ("don't worry, stay the course" with no real acknowledgment of what's happening) or manufactured panic (urgent alerts designed to drive engagement during the exact moment engagement should not be the goal).

**Emotions:** Fear, often disproportionate to actual financial exposure — a market crash is one of the few moments where a customer's emotional state and their financial reality can diverge sharply, and the product's job is to close that gap honestly, not exploit it.

**Trust risks:** Silence (no acknowledgment of what's obviously happening). Overconfidence ("this is definitely temporary" stated as fact rather than a scored, honest view). Any hint that the moment is being used to drive extra engagement or notifications.

**Opportunities:** This is the single highest-leverage trust moment available to the entire company, precisely because it's the moment competitors are most likely to get wrong. A calm, honest, specifically-relevant explanation of what's happening, what it means for this customer's actual holdings, and what the platform's own historical accuracy has been in similar past regimes — delivered without a single manufactured-urgency word — is the moment that turns a customer into a believer, if it's handled the way `TRUST_RECOVERY_PLAYBOOK.md` describes.

---

## Stage 5: First Wrong Recommendation

**Expectations:** The customer expects the platform to either quietly hope they don't notice, or to explain away the miss with excuses. They do not yet expect the platform to flag its own miss before being asked.

**Emotions:** This is the moment of maximum vulnerability for the entire trust relationship — a customer who was told to be confident and was let down is deciding, right now, whether "the platform was honest about its own confidence and still got it wrong" is a fundamentally different, more acceptable outcome than "the platform lied to me."

**Trust risks:** Any delay, minimization, or reframing of the miss. Any attempt to bury the graded outcome rather than surface it with the same visibility as a win. Silence.

**Opportunities:** Handled per `TRUST_RECOVERY_PLAYBOOK.md`, this is actually the single biggest opportunity in the entire twelve-month relationship to prove the platform's honesty claim is real, not marketing. A customer who sees a miss acknowledged plainly, attributed honestly, and folded into the visible track record without spin will trust the platform's *next* confident call more, not less — this is the counterintuitive mechanic the entire company is built around.

---

## Stage 6: First Successful Recommendation

**Expectations:** The customer expects, reasonably, to feel some satisfaction — and may also expect the platform to over-celebrate the win in a way that feels like it's trying to earn continued engagement off the back of one good call.

**Emotions:** Validation, and — if handled well — a subtle but important shift from "I hope this is right" to "I'm starting to actually trust this."

**Trust risks:** Over-celebration (confetti, exaggerated language) that reads as more interested in the customer's continued attention than in the customer's actual understanding. Treating a single win as proof of a pattern before enough evidence exists to say so honestly.

**Opportunities:** A quiet, calm acknowledgment — "this call was graded correct, here's what specifically played out as expected" — reinforces the same honest tone as every other moment in the relationship, proving the platform's restraint isn't just for bad news.

---

## Stage 7: One-Year Customer

**Expectations:** By this point, the customer's expectations are set by their own accumulated experience with the product specifically, more than by the category at large — they expect the platform to behave the way it has behaved for the prior eleven months, and any sudden change in tone, honesty, or restraint will stand out sharply against that baseline.

**Emotions:** If the prior six stages were handled well, the dominant emotion here is quiet confidence — not excitement, not loyalty in the brand-marketing sense, but the specific, durable feeling of "I know how this platform behaves, including when it's wrong."

**Trust risks:** Any drift toward more aggressive engagement tactics as the relationship "matures" — a subtle, common failure mode where a company that earned trust slowly starts spending it faster once it feels secure. A first annual report (`RETENTION_SYSTEM.md`) that undersells the misses relative to how honestly they were handled in the moment.

**Opportunities:** The annual "what we got right and wrong" report is the single moment this entire year of small trust-building or trust-eroding decisions gets consolidated into one artifact. A one-year customer who reads that report and finds it exactly as honest as every individual moment across the year was is a customer this company will very likely keep for years ten and twenty as well — this is the compounding the whole company is betting on.
